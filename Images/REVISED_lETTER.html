Fact Check: Israel's Chief Rabbi Did NOT Write A Condolence Letter To Kirk Family On September 2, 2025 -- Letter Was Written On September 12, 2025

Sarah Thompson
Updated Thu, September 18, 2025 at 8:11 PM EDT
2 min read
Add Yahoo as a preferred source to see more of our stories on Google.
Add Yahoo on Google
  • Israel's Chief Rabbi David Yosef did not write a condolence letter to the family of Charlie Kirk on September 2, 2025, as a typing error caused the date on the letter to be incorrect.
  • The letter was actually written on September 12, 2025, and the office of the Chief Rabbi confirmed that the September 2 date was a technical error in typing.
  • Despite the incorrect date, the content of the letter and the heartfelt message of condolence it conveyed remained unchanged, as clarified by the office of the Chief Rabbi.

Did Israel's Chief Rabbi, David Yosef, write a condolence letter to the family of Charlie Kirk on September 2, 2025 -- more than a week before Kirk was killed? No, that's not true: The letter was written on Sept. 12, 2025, but it was shared publicly online with a typing error -- it was dated September 2, 2025. In an email to Lead Stories, the office of the Chief Rabbi said this was a technical error in typing, and emphasized that the letter was written on September 12th.

One example of a post calling attention to the date on the letter (archived here) was published on X by @jacksonhinklle on Sept. 17, 2025. It was captioned:

🚨🇮🇱🇺🇸 BREAKING: ISRAEL'S CHIEF RABBI wrote a condolence letter about CHARLIE KIRK'S DEATH on September 2nd, 8 days BEFORE the ASSASSINATION.

The Hebrew calendar date 9 Elul, 5785 corresponds with September 2, 2025.

The letter is still LIVE on Israel Heritage Foundation (@IHF_Heritage) X account.

The post contained three images, a portrait photo of Charlie Kirk, The Israel Flag, and a screenshot (pictured below) of a now-deleted post by @EYakoby which includes the image of the condolence letter. This screenshot shows the date on the letter is typed as:

In the Bible, 9 Elul, 5785
September 2, 2025

The @jacksonhinklle post notes that the letter is still live on the Israel Heritage Foundation (@IHF_Heritage) X account. That Sept. 15, 2025 post (archived here) contains a screenshot of the same letter (pictured below) with the September 2, 2025 date, but the image extends further down to include the official seal, signature and address information at the bottom of the letter.

Lead Stories reached out to the office of the Chief Rabbi of Israel (Maran Rishon LeZion) to ask for a comment regarding the date on the letter. We received a reply (translated here by Google) on Sept. 18, 2025:

Earlier, we published the letter of condolence that the Chief Rabbi sent to Charlie Kirk's family. We wish to clarify that a technical error occurred in the office when typing the date on the letter.
We emphasize that the letter was actually written on September 12th, and this error in the date does not detract from the content of the letter or the heartfelt and meaningful message of condolence it conveyed. The updated letter, with the corrected date, is attached.

Colorado mom has her son ‘arrested’ when he refused to take first day of school photo

Owen Scott
Thu, August 13, 2026 at 11:09 AM EDT
2 min read

  • A Colorado mom had a police officer handcuff her son in a mock arrest after he refused to take a first-day-of-school photo.
  • The mom called in a local officer named Jack, who pretended to arrest the 5th-grader and even posed for pictures with him in the cop car.
  • The Keenesburg Police Department Chief praised the officers for knowing when to be firm, fair, and fun, but warned against social media pranks like the 'A.I. Homeless Man' trend.

A Colorado mom had a police officer handcuff her son in a mock arrest when he refused to take a photo on the first day of school.

After 5th-grader Colton refused to take the picture on Monday morning, Keenesburg mom Amanda Stolz says that she called in a local officer named Jack.

On Facebook, she revealed that Colton "may or may not have ended up in handcuffs before school."

"Officer Jack had to step in and inform him that refusing your mom a first-day-of-school picture is, in fact, against the law," she joked.

Stolz shared images of the 'arrest' on social media, including the moment that Jack cuffed a laughing Colton. She also shared a photograph of the cop putting Colton into the cop car.

In one image, Jack even posed with the car while Colton grinned from the backseat.

Stolz told CBS Colorado that it was "awesome that Officer Jack took it serious."

"We were like, 'Well, I guess we have you back-to-school pictures now, don't we?'" she said.

In her Facebook post, Stolz added that she was thankful for the work that Keenesburg Police officers do in the local community.

"Moments like this are something these kids will remember, and I love seeing our officers taking the time to joke around, connect with them, and make their morning a little more fun," she added.

"I love having fun with kids and showing them another side of policing," Officer Jack, who is also known as Officer Ross, told The Independent.

Keenesburg Police Department Chief Jensen that he is "exceedingly proud of our officers for knowing when to be firm, when to be fair, and when to be fun."

"Congratulations to Colton on surviving his first day of fifth grade, and to Mom for securing the evidence," he said. "Here's to more smiles, more memories, and more moments that remind us why we love this little town."

But while the police were happy to participate in this case, not every social media joke goes down so well with authorities.

Last year, cops in Michigan warned families about the 'A.I. Homeless Man prank', which saw fake images of vagrants men generated inside homes to scare family or roommates.

West Bloomfield Police Department hit out at the trend in a social media post, warning that while it may seem like a joke it could can cause panic, lead to dangerous or violent reactions and can waste emergency resources.

"Please talk to your kids about the real consequences of these trends," the department said. "What seems funny online could have serious emotional and or legal consequences."
